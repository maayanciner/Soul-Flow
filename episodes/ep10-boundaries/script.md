## THE SCRIPT

### 1. Cold open (0:00–1:00)

Have you ever said, "Sure, no problem!" with a big smile on your face… while a little voice inside you whispered, "Actually… it's a problem"?

[pause]

Maybe it was a friend asking for a favor when you were exhausted. Maybe it was saying yes to a family dinner when your body was begging for the couch. Maybe it was the hundredth time you said "I'm fine," when you were very much **not** fine.

If you just nodded, this episode is for you.

Today we're talking about boundaries. Not the cold kind, with walls and slammed doors. The warm kind. The kind that might be the most loving thing you ever do, for yourself and for every person you love.

[MUSIC: theme in, then under]

---

### 2. Welcome (1:00–2:15)

Welcome to Soul Up. I'm Maayan Ciner, and this is Episode 10.

Ten! Double digits! [laugh] In my house, that calls for cake. And today I'm going to do something I almost never did for most of my life: I'm taking the first slice. By the end of this episode, you'll understand why that's a big deal.

Here's what we'll explore together. Where people-pleasing really begins, and why it usually starts when we're very small. What science says happens in our bodies when we keep swallowing our "no." What the Rambam, Joe Dispenza and Louise Hay teach about loving ourselves first. And three real moments from my own home: a phone call, a hallway and a kitchen. Then I'll give you three simple practices, and we'll finish with a short meditation, just you and me.

So grab your tea, or your car keys, or your laundry basket. I know you're multitasking. [smile] Let's begin.

[MUSIC out]

---

### 3. The little weather reporter (2:15–3:45)

Boundaries are rarely broken in one big, dramatic moment. Usually they wear away quietly. A little yes here. A swallowed feeling there. And most of the time, we don't even notice it's happening.

For many of us, it starts in childhood.

Some kids walk into a room and look for the snacks. Other kids walk into a room and check the weather. Not the weather outside. The weather inside the people. Whose face looks tight? Who's upset? What can I do to make it better?

If you were that second kind of kid, I see you. Because I was that kid.

I wasn't just a child. I was a tiny weather reporter. "Good morning! Today's forecast: Mom looks tired, with a seventy percent chance of worry. Recommended action: be extra good, need nothing, and smile a lot."

[pause]

---

### 4. My story (3:45–6:00)

My mom raised me as a single mother. She had a very low income and no family to lean on. My dad didn't always treat her well. She was surviving, financially and emotionally, on so many levels, and she was doing it mostly alone.

And I felt it. Kids always feel it. So without anyone asking me to, I decided my job was to support her. To be easy. To be helpful. To never add one more problem to her pile.

Other teenagers had a rebellious phase. I had a helpful phase. It lasted… well, let's just say a very long time. [smile]

There was no room for me to be the wild one, the messy one, the one who needed to be held. Because I could see that the grown-up in my life needed holding too.

Now, I want to be very clear about something, because it matters to me. This is not my mother's fault. My mom was one of the most giving, loving, dedicated people I have ever known. She was loyal. She was grounded. She was someone I could always trust. She did everything in her power to make me feel good, and she gave me more than she had.

Two things can be true at the same time. I had a wonderful mother. **And** I grew up too fast.

*[optional]* Mom, if you're listening: thank you. This episode isn't about what you didn't give me. It's about what I'm finally learning to give myself.

[pause]

---

### 5. Why it happens (6:00–8:15)

Psychologists actually have a name for what I lived. It's called **parentification**. It's when a child becomes, in some way, a parent to their own parent. Sometimes it's practical: cooking, cleaning, looking after younger siblings. And sometimes it's emotional: being the comforter, the listener, the little shoulder to cry on.

Research has found a link between carrying that kind of weight as a child and struggling more as an adult. But researchers have also found something hopeful: many of these kids grow up remarkably resilient, caring and strong. So if this was you, you're not broken. You're a survivor with a very big heart.

Another idea helped me understand myself. We all know the stress responses fight, flight and freeze. Therapist Pete Walker added a fourth one: **fawn**. Fawning means keeping yourself safe by pleasing others. Making everyone happy, so nobody gets angry, nobody leaves, nobody falls apart.

For a child, that's not weakness. That's genius. It's how a small person survives in a big, uncertain world.

The problem is, the child grows up… and the program keeps running.

Dr. Joe Dispenza teaches that by our mid-thirties, about ninety-five percent of who we are is a set of memorized programs: habits, emotional reactions and beliefs, all running automatically, like software on a computer.

So my little helper didn't retire when I turned eighteen. She kept showing up for work. I was a grown woman with a home and a family, and somewhere in the background, an eight-year-old was still checking everyone's weather. Like an old app on your phone that you forgot about, quietly draining your battery all day.

And here's Dispenza's good news: a program you can see is a program you can change. Noticing it is the first step off autopilot.

---

### 6. The turning point (8:15–9:30)

Here's what finally flipped something in me.

Our survival instinct is the strongest instinct we have. As a child, survival meant pleasing everyone. As an adult, I realized survival means something completely different. It means taking care of myself. Because if I don't do it, nobody else will. Not because people don't love me, but because nobody else lives inside my body. Nobody else can feel what I need before I do.

About two thousand years ago, Hillel the Elder put it in three short questions:

"If I am not for myself, who will be for me? And if I am only for myself, what am I? And if not now, when?"

[pause]

I love this, because it holds the whole balance. Yes, be for yourself. No, not **only** for yourself. And please… don't wait as long as I did. [smile]

---

### 7. What the soul eats (9:30–11:00)

So what does "being for myself" actually look like?

A warm bed, food on the table, a roof over your head: that keeps the body alive. But your soul has its own hunger. It needs to feel safe. It needs to feel seen. It needs to feel loved. Without that, we're breathing… but we're not really living.

And here's the part nobody told me as a child: the first person who can feed that hunger, every single day, without waiting for anyone's permission, is **you**.

Louise Hay, who taught millions of people to love themselves, said: "You have been criticizing yourself for years, and it hasn't worked. Try approving of yourself and see what happens."

One of her best-known practices is mirror work. You look into your own eyes in the mirror and say, "I love you. I really love you." Out loud. Every day.

The first time you try it, you might laugh. You might cry. You might check whether the neighbors can hear you. [smile] That's okay. Nobody said healing has to look cool.

Self-love isn't a bubble bath once a year. It's the daily food of the soul, and it's the heart of all personal growth.

---

### 8. So what is a boundary? (11:00–12:45)

A boundary is simply knowing where I end and you begin. What's okay for me, and what isn't. When something starts, and when it's time for it to end.

I'm an art teacher, so let me explain it the way I'd explain it to my students.

In watercolor, there's a technique called wet-on-wet. You wet the paper, drop in some color, and it blooms and spreads everywhere. Gorgeous, for a moment. But if you keep dropping in more colors, a little red, then blue, then yellow, they all run into each other. And do you know what you end up with? **Mud.** Every art teacher's nightmare. A brown, muddy puddle.

As a child, I was wet paper. My mom's worry ran into me. The tension at home ran into me. Every feeling around me spread through me, until I honestly couldn't tell which color was mine.

That's what life without boundaries feels like. You lose your own color.

But watercolor teaches something beautiful. If you let one layer dry before you add the next, the colors stay clear. They can sit side by side. They can even overlap. And they still glow.

That drying time? That pause? That's a boundary. It doesn't keep the color out. It keeps your color **yours**.

---

### 9. What it costs the body (12:45–14:15)

When I don't set a boundary, it doesn't stay in my head. It goes straight into my body. I feel sick. I get irritable, frustrated, angry. And underneath it all, there's a lonely little feeling: nobody sees me.

Dr. Gabor Maté, a physician who spent decades studying stress and illness, wrote a whole book about this, called *When the Body Says No*. His message, in one sentence: when we never learned how to say no, our body may end up saying it for us.

And there's one study that honestly stopped me in my tracks. A large American heart study, the Framingham Offspring Study, followed thousands of adults for ten years. Women who kept quiet during conflict with their husbands, what the researchers called "self-silencing," were **four times** more likely to die during those ten years than women who spoke up.

It's one study, and the researchers themselves said it needs to be confirmed. And no, I am not telling you to go start a fight tonight. Please don't send me your marriage counselor's bill. [laugh] But it's a powerful reminder: swallowing your voice isn't free. Your body pays for it.

---

### 10. Why the people you love need you full (14:15–16:00)

Each of us has a bucket. An emotional bucket and a physical one. When I keep giving from an empty bucket, I don't end up with more love. I end up with resentment, and there's no room left for joy. And what do the people I love get? Not a warm, generous Maayan. They get a tired, short-tempered version of me who's doing everything for them… and secretly keeping score.

That doesn't help anyone.

Researcher Brené Brown found that the most compassionate people she interviewed were also the ones with the clearest boundaries. Their limits were what kept them kind instead of resentful.

And psychologist Kristin Neff studied about a hundred couples. People who were kind to themselves were described by their partners as more caring and more accepting, and less controlling and less aggressive. Self-love didn't make them selfish. It made them easier to love.

The Rambam saw this more than eight hundred years ago. In his laws about dedicating property to the Holy Temple, he writes that a person should never give away everything they own. That, he says, is not piety. It's foolishness, because they'll end up depending on others.

He was talking about money. I believe it's just as true for our energy, our time and our hearts. Giving until you're empty isn't holy. It's just… empty.

And think about the Torah's famous commandment: "Love your neighbor **as yourself**." The love you give yourself is the starting point. It's the measure for all the love you give everyone else.

---

### 11. Three scenes from my home (16:00–19:30)

Okay. Enough theory. Let me take you into my real life, because that's where boundaries actually happen. Not in books. In phone calls and hallways and kitchens.

**Scene one: the phone call.**

If you heard Episode 7, you know I feel everything. So when a friend calls with a sad story, I want to be there for her. I really do. But sometimes I'm already overflowing with my own life, and I can feel that listening is draining me instead of helping her.

The old me would keep listening. Nodding. Saying "mm-hmm" while my soul quietly left the room. And honestly? That's not being a good friend. That's being a nodding head with a phone attached. [smile]

Now I try to say something like this:

*"I love you, and I want to really be there for you. Today I'm running on empty, and you deserve someone who can listen with a full heart. Can I call you tomorrow morning?"*

It's soft. It's honest. And it's loving to both of us.

**Scene two: the hallway at six p.m.**

I've been with the kids all day. Fights, snacks, more fights, more snacks. I've been waiting for my husband to come home like he's the rescue team. [laugh] I desperately need a shower. And not a mom shower, the four-minute kind where someone knocks to ask where their other sock is. A real one.

And he walks in… exhausted. He had a hard day. He has a phone call he has to take. The kids are bouncing off the walls, his patience is gone, and the last thing he wants is bedtime duty.

So now we have two tired adults, one hallway, and the oldest competition in marriage: Who Had the Harder Day. Nobody ever wins that one. There's no trophy. There are just two people who both feel unseen.

The old me would swallow it. Skip the shower. Do bedtime. And then be quietly furious for the rest of the night.

Today I try something different. I see him, **and** I see me. I'll say:

*"I can see you're wiped out. I am too. I need fifteen minutes to shower, and then I'm all yours for bedtime."*

His feelings are real. Mine are too. I don't have to erase myself to honor him.

And yes, I'm a mother and a wife, and some nights I look at the big picture and it's clear that he needs it more. On those nights, I give, happily. But here's the difference: now it's a choice, not a default. That's what a boundary does. It turns giving from an obligation into a gift.

**Scene three: the kitchen.**

I promised myself I'd get through this whole episode without the airplane oxygen-mask speech. You've heard it from every flight attendant and every coach on Instagram. So instead, let me tell you about my kitchen.

For years, I cooked for everyone while I was starving. And let me tell you, a hungry mom with a spatula is not a loving mom. She's a hostage negotiator with a frying pan. [laugh] I wasn't cooking with love. I was cooking with a clenched jaw.

Now, a lot of the time, I stop. I take a breath. I notice: "Oh. I'm hungry." I make myself something, I eat it, and then I feed my kids. Unless one of them is truly melting down from hunger; then of course they come first. But honestly, that's rare.

Because a hungry mom cooks with her hands. A fed mom cooks with her heart.

For so many years, my default was my husband first, my kids first, everyone first, me last. And I was bitter. Not because my family did anything wrong. Because I was starving, in every sense of the word, and calling it love.

[pause]

---

### 12. Three practices (19:30–21:15)

Let's make this practical. Three small things you can start today.

**Practice one: the one-breath yes.**
Before you say yes to anything, take one breath and notice your body. A real yes feels open, light, easy. A tight jaw, a heavy chest, a little "ugh" in your stomach? That's your body saying no before your mouth says yes. Ask yourself: "Is this a yes… or is this a 'yes, so they won't be upset'?"

**Practice two: talk back to the little helper.**
When you set a boundary, guilt might show up. "They're upset because of you." "Who do you think you are?" That's the old program talking. Put your hand on your heart and answer it: "Thank you for keeping me safe when I was little. I'm a grown-up now. I can say no and still be loved." And remember: guilt doesn't mean you did something wrong. Often, it just means you did something **new**.

**Practice three: fill first.**
Write down three small things that fill your bucket. A hot shower. A meal you eat sitting down. Ten minutes outside. Five minutes with a paintbrush. Every day, take one of them before you start giving to everyone else. Not as a reward for being good. As fuel.

---

### 13. Meditation (21:15–23:15)

[MUSIC: soft and slow, under the voice]

Let's close with a short meditation. If you're driving, keep your eyes open and just listen. If you can, find a comfortable seat and gently close your eyes.

Take a slow breath in through your nose… and let it out through your mouth.

Again. In… and out.

Place one hand on your heart.

Now picture yourself as a child. Maybe five, maybe eight, maybe ten. See that little face. Notice how hard that child has been trying. How carefully they've been watching everyone. How much they've been carrying.

Walk over to that little one. Kneel down, so you're at eye level. And say, softly:

"You were so brave. It was never your job to hold the grown-ups up. You can put it down now. I've got you."

[pause]

Now wrap your arms around yourself. A real hug. Give yourself a gentle squeeze. Feel what it's like to be held by the one person who will never leave you.

[pause]

Now imagine a soft circle of light around your body. Warm. Glowing. This is your edge. Everything inside this circle is yours: your feelings, your needs, your energy. Love can still flow in and out. But now **you** decide what comes in, and how much you give.

Breathe into that circle. And say with me, quietly, in your heart:

"I can love others… and still choose me."

Once more. "I can love others… and still choose me."

And one last time. "I can love others… and still choose me."

Take one more deep breath in… and let it go.

When you're ready, slowly open your eyes.

[pause]

---

### 14. Close (23:15–24:15)

Thank you for being here with me today.

If this episode touched something in you, send it to that one friend who always says yes. You know exactly who I mean. They might need permission to take the first slice too.

You can listen to every episode, and get my free guided meditation, at **soulflowbymaayan.com/podcast**. And I'd love to hear from you. What's one boundary you want to set this week? Send me a message on Instagram and tell me.

One gentle note: if your childhood was very painful, please don't walk this road alone. A good therapist can be one of the greatest gifts you ever give yourself.

Until next time, be gentle with yourself, listen to your body… and go eat that slice of cake. [smile]

This is Maayan Ciner, and this was Soul Up.
